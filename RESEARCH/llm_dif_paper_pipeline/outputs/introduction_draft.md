# Introduction Draft

Differential item functioning is a quiet threat to substantive comparison. A scale may appear to measure the same construct across groups, waves, or social positions, while a particular item changes its working meaning for some respondents. In that case, an observed group difference may mix two things that should be kept separate: difference in the latent construct and difference in the measurement function of the item. This distinction matters sharply in multicultural panel research, where language proficiency, discrimination experience, socioeconomic position, gender, and age can all shape how respondents meet the wording of a survey item.

Psychometric research offers a large set of tools for detecting DIF. Logistic regression, item response theory, factor analytic approaches, tree-based methods, and more recent covariate-rich frameworks all help researchers ask whether item responses differ after conditioning on the latent trait. Moderated nonlinear factor analysis is especially attractive in settings with multiple background variables, because it can model impact on the latent factor and DIF in item parameters within a single measurement framework. These models are indispensable. Yet they do not remove a second task: once a candidate item is flagged, researchers still need to ask what kind of substantive mechanism could have produced the pattern, whether that mechanism is plausible, and whether the statistical signal reflects measurement noninvariance rather than real differences in the construct.

That second task is often slower than the first. Statistical screening can return a table of item-by-covariate coefficients; it cannot by itself say whether a Korean-language item is linguistically demanding, whether a discrimination item overlaps too closely with a discrimination covariate, or whether an age effect reflects response style, developmental context, or ordinary construct-relevant variation. Prior work on identifying sources of DIF has treated this explanatory step as a problem of item content, response process, and expert judgment. The need for such judgment is a strength of psychometric validation, since DIF is never identical to unfairness. It is also a bottleneck. In large item pools, the number of item-covariate pairs can exceed what researchers can carefully review by hand.

Large language models create a new, limited opportunity at this bottleneck. Their value here does not lie in replacing DIF analysis. It lies in reading item wording, construct definitions, and covariate descriptions before outcome labels are shown, then proposing which item-covariate pairs deserve closer psychometric scrutiny. A useful LLM in this setting would act as a semantic triage device. It would ask whether the wording of an item gives a respondent with a given background characteristic a different path to endorsement, after holding the latent construct fixed. The output would be a hypothesis with a direction, a confidence score, and a rationale. The claim would remain modest until tested against response data.

Recent work makes this question timely and also narrows the room for novelty. LLMs and related transformer models have already entered psychometric item generation, item review, and pre-data screening. In DIF research more directly, Maeda and Lu used encoder-based transformer models to predict empirical DIF from item text and applied explainable AI methods to identify words associated with DIF. A work-in-progress study by Li, Marchong, and Aldib compared ChatGPT judgments about DIF in reading-test items with empirical `lordif` results. These studies show that the link between language models and DIF has already begun. The remaining question is more specific: whether a generative LLM can serve as an interpretable, theory-constrained hypothesis generator in a longitudinal, multicultural, covariate-rich measurement setting, and whether its semantic judgments add anything beyond simpler text cues.

The distinction between semantic plausibility and psychometric evidence is central. LLMs are fluent machines for producing reasons. That fluency can help when an item contains a real linguistic or cultural cue. It can also mislead. A model may confuse latent trait differences with measurement-function differences. It may produce a socially plausible story for age, gender, or income even when the item wording gives no threshold-level mechanism. It may treat construct-relevant content as bias. It may amplify stereotypes under the cover of explanation. These risks are not side notes. They define the empirical question. A defensible workflow must therefore keep the LLM blind to empirical DIF labels, compare it with simple baselines, preserve raw prompts and responses, and treat every LLM rationale as a candidate for validation rather than as evidence of DIF.

This study develops and tests such a workflow using items from the Multicultural Adolescents Panel Study. The analytic unit is the item-covariate pair. For each pair, a Gemini model receives the item wording, construct context, response options, and covariate definition, then generates a threshold-DIF probability, an expected direction, a confidence rating, and a short rationale. These predictions are compared with a keyword baseline and with provisional ordinal DIF screening results. The evaluation focuses on ranking utility: average precision, precision at the top of the ranked list, and covariate-specific performance. This framing matches the intended use. The goal is to reduce the set of pairs that require close psychometric and substantive review, not to automate a final DIF decision.

The study asks four questions. First, can an LLM generate structured, parseable DIF hypotheses for multicultural panel survey items without access to empirical DIF results? Second, do its probability scores prioritize item-covariate pairs that show provisional empirical DIF better than a keyword baseline? Third, does usefulness vary by covariate, especially across discrimination experience, Korean proficiency, income, age, and gender? Fourth, where does the model fail, particularly in the boundary between latent trait difference and item threshold DIF?

A preliminary pilot reported in this project gives a cautious reason to continue. Across 24 completed pilot items, the Gemini workflow produced 109 parseable item-covariate predictions. Thirty-two pairs were positive under provisional ordinal DIF screening. The LLM achieved an overall average precision of 0.505, compared with 0.453 for the keyword baseline, and its top ten ranked candidates contained seven provisional positives. The pattern was uneven. For Korean proficiency, the LLM performed substantially better than the keyword baseline, suggesting that it may capture semantic links between language ability and item interpretation that simple markers miss. For discrimination experience, both the LLM and keyword baseline performed well, partly because relevant language often appears directly in the item text. For age, the LLM produced weak rankings, consistent with the concern that it can generate plausible developmental narratives without matching empirical DIF evidence. The strongest scale-level signal appeared in youth acculturative stress and bicultural acceptance items, while parent acculturative stress and parent self-esteem were weaker. Directional predictions were also limited. The model could often rank candidates more usefully than it could identify the empirical sign of the DIF coefficient. These early results support a qualified claim: LLMs may be useful as semantically informed DIF hypothesis generators under psychometric guardrails, while their failure modes remain part of the evidence.

The contribution of the paper is therefore methodological and diagnostic. It does not propose a new DIF detector. It proposes a workflow for placing generative LLMs before, beside, and beneath formal DIF analysis: before it, by producing blind hypotheses; beside it, by comparing those hypotheses with keyword baselines; beneath it, by requiring empirical validation before any measurement claim is made. In that workflow, a useful LLM is judged by how it helps researchers decide what to inspect next, and by how clearly its errors reveal the limits of semantic reasoning in measurement validation.
